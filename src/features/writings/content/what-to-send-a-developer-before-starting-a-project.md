You do not need a technical specification to start a conversation about a website or web application. You do need a way to explain the work you want the software to support.

A useful brief can be short. It should help a developer understand the problem, identify the important unknowns, and suggest a sensible next step.

Here is what I find useful to know.

## Explain what needs to change

Start with the current situation and the result you want.

“We need a customer portal” names a possible solution. “Customers email us to ask about order status, and staff look it up manually” explains the problem the portal might solve.

Both statements are useful, but the second opens a better discussion. It lets us consider what customers need to see, where that information lives, and whether a portal is the right first step.

If you are starting something new, describe the task a visitor or team member should be able to complete.

## Describe the people and the main journey

Who will use the result? A customer placing an order, an administrator reviewing applications, and a manager updating stock need different information and permissions.

Choose one important journey and describe it from beginning to end. For example:

> A customer browses the catalogue, requests a business account, and receives a decision after a member of staff reviews the application.

That gives us a starting point for screens, data, and responsibilities. You can leave the technical implementation open.

## Share what already exists

Include relevant website links, tools your team uses, brand materials, and examples of the current process. Explain what works well enough to keep and what gets in the way.

References are helpful when you describe what you like about them: the way a catalogue is organised, the clarity of a service page, or how a form breaks a task into steps. A reference does not need to become a design to copy.

Use redacted examples for an initial conversation. Do not send passwords, private customer records, or access tokens in a project enquiry.

## Separate launch requirements from later ideas

A first version needs a clear boundary. List what someone must be able to do at launch, then put other useful ideas in a separate list.

For an application process, submitting and reviewing an application might be essential. Advanced reporting could follow once the core workflow is working. The priorities will depend on your business, but making the distinction helps prevent every idea from becoming a launch requirement.

Mention who will provide the copy, images, product information, and approvals. Those responsibilities affect the schedule as much as implementation work does.

## Be open about budget and timing

A budget range helps frame a realistic scope. A deadline is more useful when you explain what drives it, such as an event, a contract, or an existing service ending.

If neither is settled, say that. An early discussion can identify what needs to be clarified before a responsible estimate is possible. Expect questions about dependencies and ongoing costs as well as the build itself.

## A starting brief you can use

- **The business:** What you do and who you serve.
- **The problem:** What is difficult today, or what you want to make possible.
- **The main task:** What a user should be able to complete.
- **Existing tools:** The website, systems, and materials already in place.
- **Launch priorities:** The few capabilities that matter most.
- **Constraints:** Budget range, timing, and important dependencies.

You do not need a polished answer to every point. An honest outline is more useful than a long document that hides the uncertainties.
